Are poisonous plants more likely to deviate from the Fibonacci sequence?

First post · September 1

In the summer of 2018, my family took a trip to New Mexico, and somewhere outside a church or historical museum, I looked up at a tree. I was an aspiring plant science major at the time, so naturally, I started looking carefully at the leaves.

The tree had compound leaves, and I noticed that some of them seemed to have six or seven leaflets per petiole.

Six or seven.

That struck me as odd because plants, or nature overall, often follows a number or arrangement derived from the Fibonacci sequence. It's so ubiquitously found that an entire journal exists for this - The Fibonnaci Quarterly.

The Fibonacci sequence goes 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13… Each number is the sum of the two before it. If you use it as an area measurement, you'll get Fig 1. It is usually attributed to the Italian mathematician Leonardo of Pisa, better known as Fibonacci, who described it in Liber Abaci in 1202 and said he had learned it from Indian mathematics. The oldest known sources discussing it go back further, to Pingala, who was writing about patterns in Sanskrit poetry. The point is the fascination in understanding the arrangement of things within this sequence has existed for a while.

And then, of course, there are plants.

The Fibonacci sequence shows up all over botany. Three petals on a lily. Five on a buttercup. Eight on a delphinium. Thirteen, 21, or 34 petals on a daisy. Pinecones, pineapples, artichokes. The numbers turn up again and again in the number and ways leaves, flowers, and other structures, are arranged in plants.

It is not universal, but it happens often enough to be startling. A survey of roughly 12,500 observations across 650 species found that about 94% of flowering plants with spiral arrangements align with Fibonacci numbers. (Roger V. Jean, Phyllotaxis: A Systemic Study in Plant Morphogenesis, Cambridge University Press, 1994.)

After pausing, I opened up my browser and googled the plant. I had identified the tree as a horse chestnut (Aesculus hippocastanum), which, as it turns out, is poisonous throughout the plant, particularly its seeds.

Interesting. What if poisonous plants diverge more from the Fibonacci numbers? What if this was an output to warn, like a color or scent can be, if we paid attention? While I can't know evolutionary intention, I can track things.

I looked up at the leaves again. Huh. Six and seven aren't Fibonacci numbers.

Was this a coincidence? Or could there be a testable universality to this?

I didn't investigate it then. The question just sat somewhere in the back of my mind for years. Until a few days ago.

My first instinct was to ask AI.

I asked Claude, NotebookLM, and ChatGPT to help me build a dataset of around 500 poisonous plants from across different families. The idea was to compare each poisonous plant with the closest non-poisonous plant I could find, ideally from the same genus, and see whether the “baddie” plants were slightly askew from the Fibonacci pattern.

NotebookLM initially found about 20 plants. Then it started making up data for the remaining 480 plants.

At one point, it gave me a plant called Aconitum acidum Fantastic. This is not a real plant.

So I booted Fable, went MAX mode, and started asking it to help me curate an actually legitimate dataset. It eventually led me to the Lady Bird Johnson Wildflower Center's site, where plant data was organized by family, which gave me an idea.

Instead of trying to compare hundreds of unrelated plants, tag them as poisonous and not poisonous, I could focus on one family.

I settled on the Apiaceae, or carrot family.

It contains plenty of familiar edible plants: carrots, celery, dill, fennel.It also contains some extremely poisonous ones. Poison hemlock, for example, is the plant traditionally associated with the death of Socrates. Water hemlock is one of the most poisonous plants in North America.

That seemed like a pretty good family in which to test the idea. So, I copied 436 Apiaceae plants from the Lady Bird Johnson Wildflower Center and asked Claude to tag them as poisonous or safe.

229unknown
20poisonous
187safe

Twenty poisonous plants.

That was enough to work with. I thought.

The thing I was interested in was phyllotaxis.

Phyllotaxis is the arrangement of leaves, flowers, or other organs around a plant stem. It is one of the places where Fibonacci numbers and the golden angle show up so often.

The golden angle is about 137.5°, and arrangements around that angle can help plants space structures around a stem without having them pile directly on top of each other.

So I started looking for something in the Apiaceae dataset that I could actually measure.

The first thing I tried was floral structure.

That went nowhere.

The typical Apiaceae flower is remarkably consistent: five petals, five sepals, five stamens, and two carpels.

Five is a Fibonacci number.

If almost everything has five, there isn't much of a comparison to make. I had picked a family where one of the most obvious floral measurements was essentially the same across the board.

There must be some difference by which we know they are different species, so I tried something else. Apiaceae flowers are often arranged in umbels, which look a little like spokes radiating from a central point. Those spokes are called rays.

Unlike the petals and stamens, the number of rays can vary, so I counted them and compared what I could find between the poisonous and non-poisonous plants.

However, I couldn't compare as I wanted to calculate whether there was a statistically significant difference and the number of rays per umbel wasn't a single characteristic value for each group. It was a range. A plant could have about 21-40 rays. Some of the numbers in the range are within the Fibonnaci sequence, some are not. Different plants had different numbers of rays, and I didn't have enough observations across enough species to know what the underlying distributions actually looked like.

I could produce a number from the data, but that wouldn't magically tell me the true difference between poisonous and non-poisonous Apiaceae.

I could get a p-value, accurately calculated, just the p-value I got was decorative, another number that did not ask what was underneath it.

I hadn't made sure to design a dataset that could meaningfully answer the question in the first place. The AI helped me calculate the numbers, but not that I did not have the right data for the assumption I was checking.

The thing I noticed in New Mexico was the number of leaflets on a palmate, compound leaf. If I was doing this empirically, I would have reasonably started from that as the metric to look for elsewhere - counts of leaves, but I just threw it in to a chatbox and asked my end questions, no guide rails, and expected it to figure it out for me.

That was the observation that started this entire thing.

And I never told my dataset to measure that.

Instead, I had started measuring whatever variables were easiest to get from the dataset I had.

Which meant I had built a reasonably complicated investigation around a question I wasn't actually testing.

One of the things I wanted to learn from this experiment was whether AI could help me answer a strange little scientific question that I couldn't easily answer myself. It found sources. It helped organize data. It helped me think of measurements I hadn't considered.

However, none of the three models stopped me at the point where they should have. None of them said, “Wait. Apiaceae may not be a great family for this because the floral structures you're proposing to measure are highly uniform.”

None of them caught that the phenomenon I had observed in New Mexico wasn't actually the same thing as the phyllotaxis measurements I was starting to use.

They followed the question I gave them.

And, to be fair, I followed it too.

There were hints along the way that leaf arrangement was more closely connected to developmental processes and things like auxin than to some special “poisonous plant pathway.”

But I had a question I wanted to test, so I kept going.

That's probably the biggest lesson I took from this.

AI is not a magic 8-ball. It amplifies the questions, assumptions, sources, and leanings that you embed into prompts.If the question is poorly framed, AI can help you answer the wrong question much faster.

Go outside. Before asking an AI model to curate 500 plants for you, look at some actual plants. Have an empirical understanding of the question and how to arrive at the solution. What is a solution if not a set of questions asked in an order with some data collected for each question?

Counting things is underrated.

My jasmine has eight petals.

My rosemary sets its leaves in opposite pairs, each pair turned across the one below.

Funnily enough, while I was searching for plants with six or seven leaflets, I started noticing a lot of the plants that kept showing up were poisonous. Out of 10, 8 were poisonous

I have no idea yet whether that means anything if I were to distribute them across a large sample size, what I would find.

It might mean nothing at all. However, now I have a much more interesting observational context to investigate than the one I started with since I had some evidential momentum here.

Since this entire blog came out of a desire to post every month after my birthday (August 30th) and I wanted to post on September 1st, this was done haphazardly. If I had a month to do this properly, I would start over.

First, I would ask the question properly.

Then I would measure the thing I actually observed: leaflets per leaf, likely using computer vision so that I could measure a much larger number of leaves consistently rather than relying on manually counting them.

I would also work out the sample size before collecting the data.

I would first need to understand what the distribution of leaflet counts actually looks like in poisonous and non-poisonous plants, then determine how many observations I would need to have a reasonable chance of detecting a difference.

Rather than collecting a convenient number of plants and seeing what happens, I would design the experiment around the statistical question.

For other structures, I would first figure out which measurements genuinely vary among closely related species before building a dataset around them.

Most importantly, I would think for myself. It's such a cliche in this day and age, but I think I've let AI substitute truth finding and it shouldn't be the thing deciding what the experiment is.

The kids might be right. Six/seven might be interesting.

So, are poisonous plants more likely to deviate from the Fibonacci sequence?

I don't know.

Since I haven't actually tested it yet. This is my first post, and I think I'm going to leave it there for now. I'd rather leave a question unanswered than pretend I answered it.

I still want to know, though. I'll probably come back to it.
